## 1. Executive Summary
Duckworks is using and piloting AI across engineering, customer operations, supply-chain planning, manufacturing quality, recruitment, and employee productivity. The business opportunity is significant: the current use cases are intended to shorten design cycles, improve support availability, reduce excess inventory, strengthen quality inspection, reduce recruiter workload, and improve employee productivity. At the same time, AI adoption is already occurring faster than existing governance processes can accommodate, including unregistered employee use of public generative-AI services.

The business case is therefore not for “AI compliance” as a standalone activity. It is for a scalable enterprise capability that lets Duckworks pursue AI value while controlling exposure to safety, security, privacy, fairness, operational, third-party, legal, and assurance risks. This is consistent with Duckworks’ stated desired future state of controlled innovation and risk-proportionate governance.

## 2. Business Context and Case for Change
Duckworks is a fictional European advanced-manufacturing and technology company with approximately 1,200 employees. Project W.I.N.G. is the organization’s broader digital and AI transformation program. Management expects AI to improve competitiveness, operational efficiency, customer service, engineering throughput, planning, and workforce productivity.

The governance problem emerged because AI acquisition, development, experimentation, and use are distributed across departments. Duckworks does not yet have a single enterprise mechanism that consistently identifies the system, owner, data, affected parties, third parties, applicable obligations, risk, controls, approval state, monitoring expectations, and evidence for each material AI use case.

## 3. Strategic Business Objectives
The business case supports the existing Duckworks project objectives rather than creating a parallel governance agenda. The principal outcomes are:

- Enable responsible AI adoption without imposing the same governance burden on every use case.

- Establish enterprise-wide visibility of approved, planned, pilot, third-party, and discovered shadow AI.

- Create clear ownership, decision rights, escalation, approval, and independent assurance boundaries.

- Apply consistent risk and impact assessment with explicit separation between internal risk ratings and legal classifications.

- Integrate AI governance with existing security, privacy, procurement, engineering, change, incident, enterprise-risk, and internal-audit processes.

- Require evidence for material control and approval claims so that governance decisions are auditable.

- Create management reporting that prioritizes critical risks, blocked systems, overdue actions, incidents, third-party exposure, and reassessments.

## 5. Current Risk and Governance Exposure
The readiness assessment identifies materially different risk profiles across the portfolio. DuckTalent AI is the most significant governed use case because it can influence employment opportunities. Unregistered GenAI is the most immediate governance weakness because it represents uncontrolled use across multiple unknown use cases. Generative AI systems introduce prompt-injection, retrieval-poisoning, data-leakage, excessive-permission, hallucination, and third-party risks. DuckDesign AI and WingInspect Vision can affect product quality or safety if human validation is ineffective. FeatherForecast is comparatively lower-impact but still requires data-quality, drift, monitoring, and oversight controls.

## 6. Options Considered
The following options are a Duckworks business-case evaluation. They are organizational choices, not requirements imposed by NIST, ISO, or legislation.

| **Option**                                     | **Description**                                                                                                           | **Advantages**                                                                                                   | **Limitations / Risk**                                                                                                | **Assessment**  |
|------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------|
| 0 — Do nothing / defer                         | Continue existing local experimentation and rely on general security/risk processes.                                      | Lowest immediate implementation effort.                                                                          | Fragmentation persists; shadow AI remains unmanaged; no common evidence trail; higher remediation and assurance risk. | Not recommended |
| 1 — Department-led governance                  | Each business function defines its own AI review and controls.                                                            | Fast locally; close to use-case teams.                                                                           | Inconsistent thresholds, duplicated effort, weak enterprise visibility, uneven legal/security/privacy challenge.      | Insufficient    |
| 2 — Enterprise risk-based governance           | Central standards, inventory, risk method and reporting; tiered system-specific gates integrated into existing processes. | Proportionate, auditable, scalable; preserves experimentation; enables specialist challenge and common evidence. | Requires cross-functional capacity, process integration and disciplined evidence management.                          | Recommended     |
| 3 — Highly restrictive centralized gatekeeping | Require full central approval for nearly all AI use, including low-impact experimentation.                                | Strong central control and visibility.                                                                           | Likely to slow useful experimentation, increase bypass behavior, and apply disproportionate burden.                   | Not preferred   |

## 7. Recommended Option — Risk-Based Enterprise AI Governance
Duckworks should implement Option 2: a common enterprise governance backbone with risk-proportionate lifecycle gates. This directly supports management’s requirement that lower-impact systems should not automatically receive the same level of review as employment- or safety-sensitive systems.

The recommended operating model should combine:

- Central AI inventory and intake for new, changed, purchased, embedded, experimental, and discovered AI.

- Risk and impact classification that determines the required review depth, evidence, approval authority, and monitoring cadence.

- Specialist gates for security, privacy, legal, HR, product safety, and third-party risk where relevant.

- Defined first-, second-, and third-line responsibilities, with Internal Audit remaining independent.

- Human-oversight requirements for consequential decisions, including competence, override authority, escalation, and stop authority.

- Control and evidence requirements that distinguish planned controls from implemented and evidenced controls.

- Post-deployment monitoring, significant-change triggers, incident escalation, reassessment, suspension, and retirement.

- Management dashboarding that connects business value, lifecycle, risk, treatment, evidence, and overdue actions.

## 10. Cost and Resource Considerations
The project evidence does not include budget, salary, vendor pricing, tooling costs, legal fees, or implementation effort estimates. Accordingly, this business case defines cost categories but does not fabricate financial amounts.

- Program capacity: AI Governance Lead, system owners, Risk & Compliance, Security, Privacy, Legal, HR, Procurement, Data & AI, Product Safety, and Internal Audit observation.

- Process and tooling: inventory/workflow configuration, evidence repository, dashboarding, access controls, logging, monitoring, discovery of unregistered tools, and integration with existing GRC/IT processes.

- System assurance: security testing, model/performance validation, fairness testing where relevant, human-oversight validation, supplier review, and change evidence.

- Policy and enablement: acceptable-use rules, standards, process guidance, awareness/training content, and exception handling.

- Ongoing operating cost: governance committee cadence, reassessments, monitoring, evidence refresh, incident handling, vendor renewals, and independent assurance.

Resource estimates should be completed as part of DEL-24 Implementation Roadmap and should distinguish one-time implementation effort from recurring control operation.

|-------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|

| Capability risk   | Specialist teams may lack capacity to review every AI use case.                                                         | Could create bottlenecks.                                                                 | Use proportionality, clear triage, reusable control patterns and delegated low-risk approval. |

## 12. Implementation Approach
Implementation should follow the project’s existing dependency logic rather than producing disconnected artifacts. A staged approach also allows Duckworks to protect current operations while building longer-term capability.

| **Phase**                            | **Primary Objective**                          | **Key Actions**                                                                                                                                  | **Exit Condition**                                                                                        |
|--------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Phase 1 — Stabilize and contain      | Reduce immediate unmanaged exposure.           | Enforce current release gates; contain shadow AI; confirm owners; validate critical assumptions; establish intake and committee decision routes. | Critical unmanaged conditions have owners, actions, and approved containment.                             |
| Phase 2 — Build governance backbone  | Create repeatable enterprise process.          | Operating model, RACI, control library, acceptable use, third-party due diligence, human oversight, incident and monitoring standards.           | Material AI can move through consistent lifecycle gates with evidence requirements.                       |
| Phase 3 — Operationalize and measure | Demonstrate operating effectiveness and value. | Dashboarding, reassessment cadence, benefits baselines, KPI/KRI monitoring, evidence refresh, control testing preparation.                       | Management can see value, risk, exceptions, overdue actions, and evidence status from one portfolio view. |
| Phase 4 — Assure and improve         | Independently challenge the system.            | Risk-based internal audit / assurance program; lessons learned; maturity improvements; roadmap refresh.                                          | Independent assurance can assess design and operation without owning controls.                            |

## 13. Decision Requested
**Executive management should approve the following business-case decision:**

1.  Proceed with the enterprise risk-based AI governance option as the target operating model for Project W.I.N.G.

2.  Authorize the next-phase governance deliverables and cross-functional participation required to implement the model.

3.  Maintain the current system-specific release gates until stated acceptance conditions are evidenced and formally approved.

4.  Treat DuckTalent AI and unregistered GenAI as priority risk-reduction items; do not describe either as cleared for unrestricted production.

5.  Require critical assumptions to be validated before they are relied on for residual-risk, human-oversight, safety, or legal conclusions.

6.  Establish business and governance measurement baselines before approving any quantified ROI claim.

7.  Require quarterly executive review of critical residual risk, blocked systems, business value, major dependencies, and overdue remediation, with event-driven escalation for significant incidents or material changes.

## 14. Business Case Assumptions and Boundaries
The following boundaries are essential to keep the business case defensible:

- Duckworks, all personnel, systems, data, decisions, and business circumstances are fictional.

- Only fictional, synthetic, anonymized, or public data are used in the portfolio.

- The business case relies on the current Duckworks project source set; it does not introduce real vendor due diligence, production legal opinions, certification claims, or conformity assessments.

- External laws and regulatory obligations must be confirmed per system based on intended purpose, legal role, geography, affected persons, and implementation details.

- ISO, NIST, ENISA, MITRE, OWASP, and similar sources are governance, risk, or technical references and do not independently establish legal compliance or certification.

- Duckworks internal High/Critical risk ratings are enterprise risk-management outcomes and are separate from statutory terms such as an EU AI Act “high-risk AI system.”

- Financial values are not quantified because the evidence set does not provide validated cost or benefit baselines.
