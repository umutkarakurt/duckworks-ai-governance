# Duckworks AI Governance Project — Business Scenario

## 1. Organization Background

Duckworks is a fictional European technology and advanced manufacturing company that designs, manufactures, and supports mechanical and robotic arms engineered specifically for ducks.

The company employs approximately 1,200 staff across engineering, manufacturing, software development, artificial intelligence, information technology, cybersecurity, customer operations, supply-chain management, finance, human resources, legal, risk, compliance, and internal audit.

Duckworks has traditionally operated as an engineering-focused manufacturer. However, increasing competition, rising production costs, more complex supply chains, and customer demand for intelligent products have led executive management to pursue a broader digital and AI transformation.

To coordinate this transformation, Duckworks launches **Project W.I.N.G. — Workflows, Intelligence, Next-Generation Governance**.

The program aims to introduce artificial intelligence into core operational, technical, and corporate processes while maintaining appropriate control over security, safety, privacy, legal, ethical, and operational risks.

---

## 2. Business Drivers

Duckworks expects AI to create value in several areas.

### Engineering and Product Development

Engineering teams want to reduce mechanical-arm design cycles and improve product optimization by using generative AI and computational design tools.

Duckworks therefore begins piloting **DuckDesign AI**, which assists engineers with:

- design generation;
- component optimization;
- material-selection suggestions;
- CAD-related workflows;
- mechanical simulation support;
- comparison of alternative designs.

The expected benefit is faster product development with fewer repetitive engineering tasks.

---

### Customer Operations

Duckworks' growing international customer base has increased the workload of its customer-support function.

The company is developing **QuackBot**, a generative-AI chatbot intended to answer common customer questions, retrieve technical product information, provide troubleshooting support, explain warranty conditions, and escalate complex cases to customer-service staff.

Duckworks expects QuackBot to reduce response times and improve availability of customer support.

---

### Supply Chain and Manufacturing Planning

Duckworks operates a complex supply chain involving electronic components, mechanical parts, specialist materials, and external suppliers.

The company already uses **FeatherForecast**, a predictive machine-learning system designed to forecast:

- product demand;
- inventory requirements;
- manufacturing volumes;
- component shortages;
- supplier demand.

Management expects the system to reduce excess inventory and improve operational planning.

---

### Manufacturing Quality

Duckworks wants to improve quality-control processes on production lines.

A computer-vision system called **WingInspect Vision** is being piloted to analyze manufacturing-line images and identify:

- missing components;
- assembly problems;
- cracks;
- surface defects;
- other potential quality issues.

Human inspectors remain responsible for final product acceptance.

---

### Recruitment and Workforce Management

Duckworks receives a growing number of applications for engineering, technology, manufacturing, and corporate positions.

The Human Resources function proposes **DuckTalent AI**, an AI-supported recruitment tool intended to:

- parse CVs;
- extract qualifications and experience;
- compare applicants against predefined job criteria;
- summarize applications;
- rank applicants;
- recommend candidates for human review.

Management believes the tool could significantly reduce recruiter workload.

However, DuckTalent may influence employment opportunities and therefore raises materially different concerns from many of Duckworks' other AI systems.

---

### Employee Productivity

Several business functions have begun experimenting with generative AI.

Duckworks therefore pilots **PondGPT**, an internal generative-AI assistant intended to support employees with:

- drafting;
- summarization;
- research;
- knowledge retrieval;
- coding assistance;
- meeting summaries;
- internal process questions.

The long-term objective is to provide employees with a controlled enterprise alternative to public generative-AI platforms.

---

## 3. Emerging Governance Problem

During Project W.I.N.G., management discovers that AI adoption is occurring faster than existing governance processes can accommodate.

Different departments are evaluating, purchasing, building, or using AI systems independently.

There is no single enterprise record showing:

- which AI systems are being used;
- who owns them;
- what data they process;
- which third parties are involved;
- who may be affected by their outputs;
- whether formal risk assessments have been completed;
- whether human oversight is sufficient;
- which legal or regulatory requirements may apply;
- what controls are operating;
- what evidence exists to demonstrate those controls.

The problem becomes more significant when the Information Security team discovers **unregistered employee use of public generative-AI services**.

Examples include employees using external AI tools to:

- summarize internal correspondence;
- generate software code;
- translate internal documents;
- prepare technical material;
- review customer communications;
- assist with HR documentation;
- experiment with browser-based AI extensions.

Some employees may be entering confidential company information or personal data into services that have not undergone security, privacy, procurement, or contractual review.

Duckworks now recognizes that AI risk is no longer limited to systems formally approved under Project W.I.N.G.

---

## 4. Management Challenge

Chief Executive Officer **Dr. Mallory Duckworth** strongly supports accelerated AI adoption and does not want governance processes to prevent useful experimentation.

Chief Technology Officer **Professor Archibald McDuck** similarly wants engineering and AI teams to maintain rapid development cycles.

However, other executives raise significant concerns.

**Cassandra Duckley, CISO**, identifies risks including:

- prompt injection;
- data leakage;
- insecure AI integrations;
- model and AI supply-chain risks;
- excessive access permissions;
- third-party dependency;
- AI-specific cyberattacks.

**Beatrice Van Duck, Chief People Officer**, is particularly concerned about DuckTalent AI and its potential effect on job applicants.

Her concerns include:

- unfair candidate treatment;
- discrimination;
- inappropriate ranking criteria;
- lack of explainability;
- automation bias among recruiters;
- privacy;
- insufficient opportunities for candidates to challenge decisions.

**Reginald Duckman, Chief Risk & Compliance Officer**, concludes that Duckworks lacks an enterprise-wide method for determining which AI systems require lightweight governance and which require enhanced review.

**Penelope Duckins, Head of Internal Audit**, raises an additional concern:

> “If Duckworks says that its AI systems are controlled, can the organization actually prove it?”

This exposes a broader problem: even where controls exist, evidence of their design and operation may be inconsistent.

---

## 5. Governance Mandate

Executive management therefore instructs Reginald Duckman to establish the first enterprise-wide **Duckworks AI Governance Framework**.

The governance program must be capable of answering, for every material AI use case:

1. What AI system is being used?
2. What is its intended purpose?
3. Who owns and operates it?
4. Who or what could be affected?
5. What data does it process?
6. What could go wrong?
7. What laws, standards, or internal requirements are relevant?
8. What controls are required?
9. Who approves the use case?
10. How will Duckworks monitor the system after deployment?
11. What evidence demonstrates that governance requirements are operating?
12. When must the system be reassessed, suspended, or retired?

Management also requires that the framework remain proportionate.

A low-impact forecasting system should not necessarily undergo the same level of governance as an AI system influencing employment decisions or product safety.

## 6. Initial AI Portfolio

The first governance assessment covers seven entries:

| ID | System | Primary Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| AI-001 | DuckDesign AI | Engineering design assistance |
| AI-002 | QuackBot | Customer support |
| AI-003 | FeatherForecast | Supply-chain forecasting |
| AI-004 | WingInspect Vision | Manufacturing defect detection |
| AI-005 | DuckTalent AI | Recruitment screening and ranking |
| AI-006 | PondGPT | Internal employee AI assistant |
| AI-007 | Unregistered GenAI Usage | Shadow/uncontrolled employee AI use |

These systems intentionally represent different combinations of:

- generative AI;
- predictive machine learning;
- computer vision;
- internal and externally facing systems;
- internally developed and third-party technology;
- personal and non-personal data;
- operational decision support;
- human-impacting decisions;
- product and safety implications.

---

## 7. Project Objective

The Duckworks AI Governance Project is established to design and demonstrate a practical governance model for managing this portfolio.

The project will develop:

- an AI system inventory;
- an AI governance operating model;
- an AI Governance Committee;
- defined accountability and RACI structures;
- an AI Risk Classification and Assessment Methodology;
- AI impact assessments;
- system-specific risk assessments;
- a control framework;
- policy and procedural requirements;
- third-party AI governance requirements;
- evidence and assurance expectations;
- monitoring metrics;
- risk and governance dashboards;
- an implementation roadmap.

The project is not intended to certify Duckworks against any particular standard or provide a legal compliance determination.

Its purpose is to establish an auditable, risk-based governance foundation that can support responsible AI adoption.

---

## 8. Desired Future State

At the end of the governance transformation, Duckworks intends to operate under the principle:

> **No material AI without an owner, no owner without accountability, no material risk without treatment, and no control without evidence.**

Employees should still be able to experiment with AI, but experimentation must occur within defined boundaries.

Higher-impact systems should receive progressively stronger governance, including enhanced documentation, testing, approval, human oversight, monitoring, and independent assurance.

The intended outcome is therefore not maximum restriction.

It is **controlled innovation**: allowing Duckworks to gain business value from artificial intelligence while maintaining reasonable assurance that significant impacts are understood, governed, monitored, and accountable.
